5 Years of AI

What changed in program management for research administration, and what it means for your office.

In about five years, AI and data science tools went from a specialist research topic to everyday infrastructure in research administration. Project management platforms ship with AI assistants, institutions host their own AI platforms, and staff with little programming background build working tools. This page walks through what changed, what didn't, and how to manage it in a pre-award office, from solicitation review to submission.

    Objective 1 · Define

    Key terms

    Plain-language definitions for words you'll hear in this session. No technical background needed.

    Generative AI
    AI that creates new text, images or other content in response to a request, such as drafting an email or summarizing a document.
    Large language model (LLM)
    The technology behind tools like ChatGPT, Copilot and Claude. It's trained on large amounts of text to predict and produce language.
    Prompt
    The instructions or question you type into an AI tool. Clearer prompts usually get more useful results.
    Hallucination
    When an AI tool states something false with confidence, like a deadline or page limit that isn't in the solicitation. This is why people check the output.
    Built-in AI feature
    An AI assistant added to software you already use, such as ClickUp, Monday or an eRA system, often through a vendor update.
    Institutionally hosted AI platform
    An AI tool your university provides and manages, with rules about which data you may use in it.
    AI-assisted tool building
    Using AI to help create a working tool, such as a tracker, form or spreadsheet, without being a programmer.
    Human review
    A person checks AI output before it's used or sent. Sometimes called "human in the loop."
    Data classification
    Your institution's categories for how sensitive information is, from public to restricted. It decides which tools can touch which data.
    Security review
    IT's evaluation of a tool before approval: where data goes, who can see it, and whether the vendor uses it to train its AI.
    Pilot
    A small, time-limited test of a tool on one real workflow, with a clear question about whether it's worth keeping.
    Adoption
    People actually using a tool as part of their regular work. Buying licenses is not adoption.

        Five years at a glance

        And what stayed stubbornly the same

        The hard parts are still about people and process

        New tools didn't remove these problems. In some offices they made them more visible.

        • Change is still a people problemA tool nobody is expected to use doesn't get used.
        • Unclear ownership still stalls workIf nobody owns a process, adding AI to it won't fix that.
        • Data quality still limits resultsAI summaries are only as good as the records behind them.
        • Approvals still take timeSecurity and procurement reviews didn't speed up because the tools did.
        • Judgment still sits with peopleSomeone is still accountable for what gets submitted to a sponsor.
        • Capacity is still thinLearning a new tool competes with proposal deadlines that never stopped.

            Objective 4 · Plan

            Measuring success beyond on time and on budget

            Delivering the tool is the first step, not the finish line. Funders increasingly ask what happened next.

            Delivered
            • Tool, code and documentation released
            • On time and on budget
            Used
            • Active users over time
            • Share of eligible tasks done with the tool
            Trusted
            • User satisfaction
            • How often users redo or override the output
            Accurate
            • Share correct on real test cases
            • How serious the errors are
            Saves time
            • Minutes per task compared with the old way
            • Turnaround time for requests
            Lasts
            • A named owner after the project ends
            • Documentation and a maintenance plan

              Conclusion

              Key takeaways

              From section 2

              AI went from a specialist research topic to everyday infrastructure in about five years. The hard parts are still people and process.

              From section 3

              Buying a tool isn't adopting it. Name an owner, set expectations and measure value.

              From section 3

              IT and compliance are partners, not a final checkpoint. Bring them in early.

              From section 4

              Programs that build AI and data tools start with users and data, evaluate honestly, and plan for life after the grant.

              Who's on the team, and what the program manager does with each

              Research administratorsUsers and subject experts

              Bring them in from discovery through evaluation, and protect their time to take part.

              Developers and data scientistsBuild the tool

              Turn user needs into clear priorities, and shield them from constant scope changes.

              Research software engineersMake it last

              Plan for prototypes to become maintainable, documented software.

              Data owners and stewardsControl the inputs

              Identify them early and start access approvals at kickoff.

              IT, security and complianceApprove and host

              Treat them as partners and put their reviews in the schedule.

              EvaluatorsMeasure results

              Agree on measures early and make data available for evaluation.

              Partner institutionsShare the work

              Align timelines, agreements and communication across sites.

              Leadership and sponsorsFund and champion

              Report outcomes, such as who uses the tool and what changed, not only outputs.
